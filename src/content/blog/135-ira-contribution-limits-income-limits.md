## The Contribution Limit Itself

For 2025, you can contribute up to **$7,000** to IRAs (Traditional and Roth combined) if you're under 50, or **$8,000** if you're 50 or older. This limit applies per person, not per account — if you have both a Traditional and Roth IRA, your combined contributions across both can't exceed this total.

## Roth IRA Income Phaseouts

Unlike the contribution limit itself, your ability to contribute to a **Roth** IRA phases out as your income rises. The IRS adjusts the exact thresholds each year based on your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) and filing status — once you're in the phaseout range, your allowed contribution shrinks gradually; above the top of the range, it drops to zero.

## Traditional IRA Deduction Phaseouts

A Traditional IRA contribution is always *allowed* regardless of income — the question is whether it's **deductible**:

- If neither you nor your spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan, your contribution is fully deductible no matter your income.
- If you **are** covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out at moderate income levels.
- If you're not covered but your spouse **is**, a separate, higher phaseout range applies to you.

## What Happens If You're Phased Out of Both?

If your income is too high for a deductible Traditional IRA contribution and too high for a direct Roth contribution, you can still make a **nondeductible Traditional IRA contribution** and convert it to Roth — commonly called a "backdoor Roth."

## Worked Example

A single filer earning $50,000 with no workplace retirement plan contributes the full $7,000 to a Traditional IRA — fully deductible, reducing taxable income to $43,000. The same filer earning $50,000 with full Roth eligibility could instead put $7,000 into a Roth IRA with no deduction now, but tax-free growth forever.

## Excess Contribution Penalties

Contributing more than your limit (or more than you're eligible for under the phaseout) triggers a **6% excise tax per year** on the excess amount until it's corrected — so it pays to double-check your eligibility before contributing, especially mid-year if your income changes.

## Spousal IRAs

A non-working or lower-earning spouse can still contribute up to the full limit to their own IRA, as long as the couple's combined earned income covers both contributions — a valuable option for single-income households.
