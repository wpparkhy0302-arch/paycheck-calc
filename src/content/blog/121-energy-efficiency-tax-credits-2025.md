## Two Different Credits, Often Confused

Federal energy tax incentives for homeowners split into two separate credits with different rules: the **Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit** (for smaller upgrades like insulation and windows) and the **Residential Clean Energy Credit** (for solar, geothermal, and battery storage). Mixing them up leads to miscalculated expectations about how much you'll actually get back.

## Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit

This credit covers 30% of the cost of qualifying improvements, subject to **annual** limits (it resets each year, unlike a lifetime cap):

| Improvement | Annual credit cap |
|---|---|
| Exterior windows/skylights | $600 |
| Exterior doors | $250 per door, $500 total |
| Insulation and air sealing | 30% of cost, no specific cap beyond the overall limit |
| Heat pumps, heat pump water heaters, biomass stoves | $2,000 |
| Overall annual limit (combined) | $1,200 (plus the separate $2,000 heat pump bucket) |

Because the heat pump category has its own separate $2,000 bucket on top of the $1,200 general limit, a homeowner installing a heat pump plus new windows and a door in the same year could claim up to $3,200 total in one tax year.

## Residential Clean Energy Credit

This credit covers **30% of the full cost** (equipment plus installation) of solar panels, solar water heaters, geothermal heat pumps, small wind energy systems, fuel cells, and battery storage systems (3 kWh capacity or more) — with **no annual or lifetime dollar cap**. Unused credit carries forward to future tax years if it exceeds your tax liability in the year of installation.

**Example:** A $24,000 solar panel installation generates a $7,200 credit (30% × $24,000). If your tax liability that year is only $5,000, you use $5,000 of the credit and carry forward the remaining $2,200 to next year.

## What Doesn't Qualify

- Improvements to a rental property you don't live in (different rules apply for landlords, generally as business expenses)
- Used/previously-installed equipment
- Standard replacement HVAC that doesn't meet efficiency certification requirements — check for the applicable CEE or ENERGY STAR tier required for that specific equipment type

## Documentation You'll Need

Keep the manufacturer's certification statement and receipts showing the equipment met the efficiency requirements in effect for the year of installation — requirements have tightened over time, so a model that qualified a few years ago might not qualify now.

## These Are Credits, Not Deductions

Unlike a deduction that reduces taxable income, these are **credits** that reduce your tax bill dollar-for-dollar. A $2,000 credit is worth $2,000 regardless of your tax bracket — more valuable per dollar than a $2,000 deduction, which saves you only your marginal rate times $2,000.
