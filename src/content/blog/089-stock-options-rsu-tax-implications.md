## Equity Comp Has Its Own Tax Playbook

Stock options and RSUs (Restricted Stock Units) are increasingly common parts of total compensation, but each is taxed at a fundamentally different point in time — and getting the timing wrong is one of the most expensive mistakes employees make.

## RSUs: Taxed the Moment They Vest

RSUs are taxed as **ordinary W-2 wage income** on the day they vest, based on the stock's fair market value that day — regardless of whether you sell the shares. Your employer typically withholds taxes by automatically selling ("sell-to-cover") a portion of the newly vested shares.

**Worked example:** 100 shares vest when the stock is worth $80/share.

- Taxable income added to your W-2: 100 × $80 = $8,000.00
- Federal supplemental withholding (22% flat, common for equity comp): $1,760.00
- Shares typically withheld to cover tax: ~22 shares (varies by total withholding rate including FICA and state)

If you later sell the remaining shares, any gain or loss from the $80 vesting price is a separate **capital gain or loss**, short- or long-term depending on how long you held them after vesting.

## Non-Qualified Stock Options (NSOs): Taxed at Exercise

NSOs create ordinary income at **exercise**, equal to the difference between the exercise (strike) price and the fair market value at exercise — the "bargain element."

**Worked example:** Option to buy at $20/share; stock is worth $50/share at exercise, 500 shares.

- Bargain element: ($50 − $20) × 500 = $15,000.00
- This $15,000 is added to W-2 wages and subject to federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare in the year of exercise.

## Incentive Stock Options (ISOs): The Special Case

ISOs create **no regular income tax** at exercise — but the bargain element may trigger the **Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)**. If you hold the shares at least 2 years from grant and 1 year from exercise, the eventual sale is taxed entirely as long-term capital gains, often the best tax outcome of all equity types — but a disqualifying early sale converts part of the gain back to ordinary income.

## Quick Comparison

| Type | Taxed At | Tax Type |
|---|---|---|
| RSU | Vesting | Ordinary income (W-2) |
| NSO | Exercise | Ordinary income (W-2) |
| ISO | Sale (if holding periods met) | Long-term capital gains |

## The Underwithholding Trap

Equity compensation is frequently under-withheld relative to your actual marginal tax rate, especially for high earners — many people owe a surprise balance in April from vested RSUs taxed at the flat 22% rate when their real bracket is 32% or higher. Consider adjusting your W-4 or making an estimated payment in the quarter equity vests.
