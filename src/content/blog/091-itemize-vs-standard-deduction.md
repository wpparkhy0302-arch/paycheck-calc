## The Default Choice Most Filers Should Take

Roughly 9 out of 10 taxpayers take the standard deduction rather than itemizing — and for most people, that's the right call. Since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act nearly doubled the standard deduction starting in 2018, itemizing only pays off if your deductible expenses clear a fairly high bar.

## 2025 Standard Deduction Amounts

| Filing Status | Standard Deduction |
|---|---|
| Single | $15,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $30,000 |
| Head of Household | $22,500 |
| Married Filing Separately | $15,000 |

Filers 65 or older, or blind, get an additional $1,550–$1,950 on top of these amounts.

## What You Can Itemize Instead

Itemized deductions, reported on **Schedule A**, include:
- **State and local taxes (SALT)** — capped at $10,000 total for income, sales, and property taxes combined
- **Mortgage interest** on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt
- **Charitable contributions** — cash gifts up to 60% of AGI, property up to 30%
- **Unreimbursed medical expenses** exceeding 7.5% of your AGI
- **Casualty losses** in federally declared disaster areas

## The Math: When Itemizing Wins

Itemizing only helps if your total Schedule A deductions exceed your standard deduction. A homeowner filing Single with $9,000 in mortgage interest, $10,000 in SALT (capped), and $3,000 in charitable gifts totals $22,000 in itemized deductions — well above the $15,000 standard deduction, saving them roughly $1,540 at a 22% marginal rate ($7,000 × 22%).

By contrast, a renter with no mortgage and modest charitable giving of $1,500 has almost no path to beating a $15,000 standard deduction and should simply take it.

## The SALT Cap Changed the Calculus

Before 2018, state income and property taxes were fully deductible, which pushed many middle- and upper-middle-income homeowners into itemizing. The $10,000 SALT cap (unchanged through 2025) means homeowners in high-tax states like New York, New Jersey, and California often hit the cap on SALT alone and need substantial additional mortgage interest or charitable giving to clear the standard deduction threshold.

## "Bunching" Strategy

Some taxpayers who are close to the itemizing threshold use **bunching**: concentrating two years' worth of charitable donations or elective medical procedures into a single calendar year to itemize in that year, then taking the standard deduction the next year. Donor-advised funds make this easier by letting you take the deduction immediately while distributing the actual gifts over time.

## You Don't Have to Pick the Same Way as Your State

Some states require your state itemizing choice to match your federal choice; others let you itemize on your state return even if you took the federal standard deduction. Check your state's specific rule before assuming they're linked.
