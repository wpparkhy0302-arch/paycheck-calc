## Not All Disability Income Is Taxed the Same Way

Disability benefits come from several different sources, and the taxability of each one depends entirely on **who paid the premiums** — not on the fact that it's disability income.

## Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI)

SSDI is taxed under the **same rules as regular Social Security retirement benefits** (see the "combined income" formula below). Whether any of it is taxable depends on your total income from all sources, not on the fact that it's disability-related.

## Supplemental Security Income (SSI)

**SSI is never taxable.** It's a needs-based program (not funded by your payroll taxes) for people with limited income and resources, and the IRS does not tax it under any circumstances.

## Employer-Paid Disability Insurance

If your **employer pays the premiums** for a short-term or long-term disability policy and doesn't include the premium cost in your taxable wages, then any benefits you later receive from that policy are **fully taxable** as ordinary income.

## Employee-Paid Disability Insurance

If **you** pay the premiums yourself with after-tax dollars (common for supplemental private policies), the benefits you receive are **entirely tax-free** — you already paid tax on the money used to buy the coverage.

| Who Pays Premiums | Benefits Taxable? |
|---|---|
| Employer pays, pre-tax to employee | Fully taxable |
| Employee pays with after-tax dollars | Tax-free |
| Split (employer pays part, employee pays part) | Prorated — taxable portion matches employer-paid share |

## The Combined Income Formula for SSDI/Social Security

To determine whether SSDI (or regular Social Security) is taxable, the IRS uses a **combined income** calculation:

**Combined Income = Adjusted Gross Income + Nontaxable Interest + 50% of Social Security Benefits**

| Filing Status | Combined Income | Taxable Portion |
|---|---|---|
| Single | Below $25,000 | 0% |
| Single | $25,000–$34,000 | Up to 50% |
| Single | Above $34,000 | Up to 85% |
| Married Filing Jointly | Below $32,000 | 0% |
| Married Filing Jointly | $32,000–$44,000 | Up to 50% |
| Married Filing Jointly | Above $44,000 | Up to 85% |

No more than **85%** of your benefits are ever taxable, no matter how high your other income is.

## Workers' Compensation

Workers' compensation for a job-related injury or illness is generally **fully tax-free**, with one nuance: if you also receive SSDI and the combination exceeds certain limits, part of your workers' comp can effectively make more of your SSDI taxable through an "offset" calculation.

## Example

A single filer receives $16,000 in SSDI for the year and has $20,000 in other income (a part-time job). Combined income = $20,000 + $8,000 (half of SSDI) = $28,000 — above the $25,000 threshold, so up to 50% of the SSDI ($8,000) becomes taxable, added to their $20,000 in regular income.
