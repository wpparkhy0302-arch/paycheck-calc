## The Status Most Married Couples Should Use

Married Filing Jointly (MFJ) combines both spouses' income, deductions, and credits onto a single Form 1040. For the vast majority of married couples, it produces a lower combined tax bill than filing separately — which is why roughly 95% of married filers choose it.

## Who Can File MFJ

You can file jointly if you were legally married as of **December 31** of the tax year, even if you married on New Year's Eve. If your spouse died during the year, you can still generally file MFJ for that year.

## 2025 MFJ Tax Brackets

| Taxable Income | Rate |
|---|---|
| $0 – $23,850 | 10% |
| $23,851 – $96,950 | 12% |
| $96,951 – $206,700 | 22% |
| $206,701 – $394,600 | 24% |
| $394,601 – $501,050 | 32% |
| $501,051 – $751,600 | 35% |
| Over $751,600 | 37% |

The MFJ standard deduction is $30,000 — exactly double the Single amount, which is why combining incomes rarely produces a worse result than two Single returns would.

## Benefits Unique to MFJ

- **Full access to credits**: Earned Income Tax Credit, education credits (American Opportunity and Lifetime Learning), and the Child and Dependent Care Credit are reduced or eliminated under MFS but fully available under MFJ.
- **Higher phase-out thresholds** for IRA deduction eligibility, the Child Tax Credit, and the Saver's Credit.
- **Capital loss and passive loss rules** apply more favorably combined than split between two MFS returns.

## The "Marriage Penalty" (and Bonus)

When both spouses earn similar, moderate incomes, MFJ brackets are exactly double the Single brackets, so there's usually no penalty. But when one spouse earns significantly more than the other, MFJ often produces a **marriage bonus** — the lower earner's income gets taxed starting at 10% again rather than stacking on top of the higher earner's bracket. The reverse "penalty" can appear for two high, similar earners once combined income pushes into the 35–37% brackets, which kick in at lower combined thresholds than 2× the Single thresholds at the top end.

## Worked Example

Spouse A earns $90,000, Spouse B earns $30,000 — combined $120,000.
- Taxable income: $120,000 − $30,000 standard deduction = $90,000
- Entirely within the 10%, 12%, and 22% brackets, with only the top slice taxed at 22%
- Filed separately, Spouse A alone would have $75,000 of taxable income after their own $15,000 deduction, pushing more of their income into the 22% bracket sooner — MFJ comes out ahead here.

## Joint and Several Liability

The tradeoff: both spouses are **jointly and severally liable** for the full tax bill, even if only one spouse earned the income or made an error. The **Innocent Spouse Relief** provision (Form 8857) exists specifically for cases where one spouse is unfairly held liable for the other's tax mistakes or fraud.
