## The Credit for Working Parents Who Pay for Care

Unlike the Child Tax Credit, which is about simply having a qualifying child, the **Child and Dependent Care Credit** exists specifically to offset what you spend on care so you (and your spouse, if married) can work or look for work. It covers daycare, before/after-school programs, day camp, and in-home care for a qualifying person.

## Who Qualifies as a Care Recipient

- A child under age 13 whom you claim as a dependent, **or**
- A spouse or dependent of any age who is physically or mentally incapable of self-care and lives with you more than half the year

## How the Credit Is Calculated

You can count up to **$3,000 of care expenses for one qualifying person**, or **$6,000 for two or more**. The percentage you can claim depends on your adjusted gross income:

| AGI | Credit Rate |
|---|---|
| $0 – $15,000 | 35% |
| $43,000+ | 20% (the floor for most middle- and higher-income filers) |

The rate steps down by 1 percentage point for each $2,000 of AGI above $15,000, bottoming out at 20%.

## Worked Example

A single parent earning $60,000 AGI pays $5,000/year for after-school care for one 9-year-old. Since AGI is above $43,000, the rate is 20%. Eligible expenses are capped at $3,000 for one child, so the credit is 20% × $3,000 = **$600**.

If that same parent had two children in care and spent $7,000, the expense cap rises to $6,000, making the credit 20% × $6,000 = **$1,200**.

## Both Spouses Must Have Earned Income

For married couples, both spouses generally need earned income (wages, salary, self-employment) during the period care was provided — one spouse being a full-time student or disabled counts as an exception, with their "earned income" imputed for the calculation.

## Dependent Care FSA vs. the Credit

Many employers offer a **Dependent Care FSA**, letting you set aside up to $5,000 pre-tax for care expenses. You cannot double-dip: any amount run through an FSA reduces the expense cap available for this credit dollar-for-dollar. For most middle-income families, the FSA's upfront tax savings (avoiding ~22-32% combined federal/FICA tax) beats the 20% credit rate — but if your employer doesn't offer an FSA, the credit is your main option.

## How to Claim It

File **Form 2441** with your Form 1040. You'll need the care provider's name, address, and Taxpayer ID (or SSN if an individual) — the IRS will reject the credit without this identification.
