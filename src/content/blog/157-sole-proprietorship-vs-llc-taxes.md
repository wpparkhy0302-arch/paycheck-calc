## The Surprising Tax Truth: By Default, They're Taxed the Same

Many new business owners assume forming an LLC automatically changes how they're taxed. In reality, a **single-member LLC** is taxed by default exactly like a **sole proprietorship** — both report business income on Schedule C, and both pay the same 15.3% self-employment tax on net profit. The LLC changes your *legal liability protection*, not your default *tax treatment*.

## What Actually Stays the Same

| Factor | Sole Proprietorship | Single-Member LLC (default) |
|---|---|---|
| Tax form | Schedule C | Schedule C |
| Self-employment tax | 15.3% on net profit | 15.3% on net profit |
| Separate business tax return | No | No (unless electing S-Corp/C-Corp) |
| Personal liability for business debts | Yes, unlimited | No — LLC shields personal assets |

## What Actually Changes

The real benefit of an LLC is **liability protection** — if your business is sued or can't pay a debt, your personal assets (house, personal savings) are generally shielded, whereas a sole proprietor is personally exposed. This is a legal distinction, not a tax one.

## Where Taxes *Can* Diverge: Electing a Different Tax Status

An LLC has a tax flexibility a sole proprietorship doesn't: it can **elect** to be taxed as an S-Corporation or C-Corporation by filing the appropriate IRS form, while a sole proprietorship has no such option (you'd need to actually incorporate).

**Example:** An LLC earning $100,000 in net profit, taxed by default, owes SE tax on the full $100,000. The same LLC electing S-Corp status might pay the owner a $55,000 "reasonable salary" (subject to payroll tax) and take $45,000 as a distribution (not subject to SE tax) — potentially saving several thousand dollars in SE tax, at the cost of added payroll and corporate filing complexity.

## Multi-Member LLCs Are Different Again

A multi-member LLC is taxed by default as a **partnership**, filing Form 1065 and issuing each member a Schedule K-1, rather than each member filing their own Schedule C. This is a meaningfully different filing process from a single-member LLC.

## State-Level Costs Vary Significantly

Forming an LLC often comes with state filing fees and, in some states, an annual franchise tax or fee — California, for example, charges an $800 minimum annual LLC tax regardless of profit. A sole proprietorship generally has no equivalent state-level entity fee.

## Which Should You Choose?

- **Sole proprietorship**: simplest, cheapest, no legal separation from personal assets
- **LLC (default taxation)**: same tax bill as sole proprietorship, but with liability protection and state filing costs
- **LLC electing S-Corp**: potential SE tax savings at higher income levels, with added payroll/compliance overhead
