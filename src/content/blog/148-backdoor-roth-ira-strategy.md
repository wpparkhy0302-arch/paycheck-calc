## The Problem It Solves

Roth IRAs phase out at higher income levels — above certain thresholds, you can't contribute directly at all. The "backdoor Roth" is a legal workaround that lets high earners get money into a Roth IRA regardless of income, by routing through a step the IRS income limits don't restrict.

## The Two-Step Process

**Step 1**: Contribute to a **nondeductible Traditional IRA**. Anyone can do this regardless of income — there's no income limit on making a *nondeductible* contribution, only on *deducting* one or contributing directly to a Roth.

**Step 2**: **Convert** that Traditional IRA balance to a Roth IRA. Conversions have no income limit either. Because you already paid tax on the money you contributed (it was nondeductible), you owe no additional tax on the conversion — assuming you have no other pre-tax IRA money, as explained below.

## Worked Example

A high earner contributes $7,000 to a new, empty Traditional IRA (nondeductible, since their income is too high to deduct it). A few days later, they convert the full $7,000 to a Roth IRA. Since the $7,000 basis was already after-tax, and there was no growth yet, the conversion triggers **$0** in additional tax.

## The Pro-Rata Rule: The Biggest Trap

If you have **any other pre-tax money** in Traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs, the IRS doesn't let you cherry-pick only the nondeductible dollars to convert tax-free. Instead, a **pro-rata rule** requires you to treat the conversion as proportionally pre-tax and after-tax, based on your *total* IRA balance across all accounts.

### Pro-Rata Example

You have $93,000 of pre-tax money already in a Traditional IRA, and you add a new $7,000 nondeductible contribution, for a $100,000 total balance (93% pre-tax, 7% after-tax). If you convert $7,000, the IRS treats **93% of that conversion ($6,510) as taxable**, even though you intended to convert only your new after-tax contribution.

## How to Avoid the Pro-Rata Problem

If your employer's 401(k) plan accepts incoming rollovers, you can roll your existing pre-tax Traditional IRA balances **into the 401(k)** before doing the backdoor Roth — 401(k) balances aren't counted in the pro-rata calculation, leaving your Traditional IRA balance at $0 pre-tax, so your subsequent nondeductible contribution converts cleanly.

## Timing Considerations

Many people convert quickly after contributing, specifically to minimize any investment growth between the contribution and conversion — growth that **would** be taxable, even on an otherwise clean backdoor Roth.
