## Where You Live Can Change Your Tax Bill More Than a Raise Would

State income tax rates range from **0%** in nine states to over **13%** in California at the top bracket — a bigger swing than most salary negotiations will ever produce. A cross-state move deserves real tax planning, not just a change-of-address form.

## Establishing Domicile — It's Not Just Where You Sleep

States determine tax residency based on **domicile** — your permanent, primary home — not simply where you happen to be physically on a given day. Key factors states look at include:

- Where you're registered to vote
- Where your driver's license and vehicle registration are issued
- Where you spend the majority of the year (commonly a **183-day** threshold)
- Location of your primary home, family ties, and financial accounts

Simply renting an apartment in a no-tax state while maintaining your real life in a high-tax state is a common audit target — several states (notably New York and California) are aggressive about pursuing "domicile audits" for high earners who claim to have moved.

## Part-Year Returns

If you move mid-year, you'll typically file **part-year resident returns** in both your old and new state, each taxing only the income earned (or in some states, received) while you were a resident there. Keep careful records of your move date and where income was earned.

## Remote Work Complicates Things

If you work remotely for an employer based in a different state than where you live, a few states apply a **"convenience of the employer" rule** — taxing your income based on your employer's location unless your remote work was required by the employer (not just your personal preference). This can result in theoretically owing tax to two states, though most states offer a credit for taxes paid to another state to prevent full double taxation.

## Example

Someone moves from New York (top rate 10.9%) to Florida (no income tax) on July 1st, earning $60,000 in each state for the year. They'd file a New York part-year return reporting roughly $60,000 and owe New York tax on that portion, while reporting $0 state tax liability on the Florida-earned portion — but if their employer is a New York company and considers the remote arrangement a personal convenience rather than a business necessity, New York's convenience rule could still tax the "Florida" portion too.

## Moving Checklist for Tax Purposes

| Task | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Update your W-4 and state withholding form with your employer | Ensures correct state withholding going forward |
| Change driver's license and voter registration | Supports your domicile claim |
| Keep a moving date record and lease/closing documents | Needed for part-year return allocation |
| Check "convenience of employer" rules if working remotely | Avoids double state taxation surprises |
