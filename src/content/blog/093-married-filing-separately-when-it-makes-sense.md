## The Status Most Advisors Tell You to Avoid — Usually for Good Reason

Married Filing Separately (MFS) almost always results in a higher combined tax bill than filing jointly, because it strips away access to several of the tax code's most valuable credits. But in a handful of specific situations, MFS genuinely saves money or protects one spouse from the other's liabilities — it's a niche tool, not a mistake by default.

## What You Lose Under MFS

- **Earned Income Tax Credit** — completely disallowed
- **American Opportunity and Lifetime Learning education credits** — completely disallowed
- **Child and Dependent Care Credit** — generally disallowed
- **Student loan interest deduction** — completely disallowed
- **IRA deduction** — phases out at much lower income than MFJ
- **Standard deduction** — only $15,000 each (not the $30,000 combined MFJ gets), and if one spouse itemizes, the other **must** itemize too, even with few expenses

## When MFS Actually Helps

**1. Income-driven student loan repayment.** Under plans like SAVE, IBR, or PAYE, your monthly payment is based on your income — and filing MFS can exclude your spouse's income from that calculation in several plans, substantially lowering the loan payment even though it costs a bit more in taxes.

**2. Large unreimbursed medical expenses.** Medical expenses are deductible only above 7.5% of AGI. Splitting income lowers each spouse's individual AGI, which can push a specific spouse's medical costs above that smaller threshold when they wouldn't clear it against the couple's combined AGI.

**3. Separating liability.** If one spouse has unreported income, back taxes, a pending audit, or you suspect fraud, MFS keeps you from being jointly and severally liable for their tax bill.

**4. Divorce in progress.** Couples separating but not yet legally divorced by December 31 sometimes prefer MFS to keep finances cleanly separated during a contentious split, even at a tax cost.

## A Worked Example

A couple earning $70,000 (Spouse A) and $60,000 (Spouse B), with $40,000 in medical bills from Spouse B's surgery:
- Combined AGI: $130,000 → 7.5% threshold = $9,750 → deductible medical expense = $30,250
- Spouse B alone, AGI $60,000 → 7.5% threshold = $4,500 → deductible medical expense = $35,500

Here MFS could unlock an additional $5,250 of deductible expense for Spouse B alone — enough in some cases to offset the lost MFJ-only credits.

## The Default Rule of Thumb

Calculate your taxes both ways before deciding. Most tax software and professionals can run a "what-if" comparison. Absent a specific reason from the list above, MFJ saves money for the overwhelming majority of married couples.
