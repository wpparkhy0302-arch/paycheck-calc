## Receiving a Gift Is Never Taxable Income to You

This surprises a lot of people: if someone gives you cash, a car, or any other gift, you owe **$0 in income tax** on it, no matter the amount. Gift tax, if it applies at all, is the **giver's** responsibility — not yours.

## The Annual Exclusion

For 2025, anyone can give up to **$19,000 per recipient** per year without any gift tax reporting requirement at all. A married couple can combine their exclusions to give $38,000 to one person per year with no paperwork.

| Scenario | Gift Tax Consequence |
|---|---|
| You receive $10,000 from a parent | No tax, no reporting, for either of you |
| You receive $50,000 from a parent in one year | Giver must file Form 709 (gift tax return); you still owe nothing |
| Giver's lifetime gifts exceed $13.99 million | Giver may owe actual gift tax — extremely rare |

## Why the Giver Rarely Pays Anything Either

Gifts above the $19,000 annual exclusion simply reduce the giver's **lifetime gift and estate tax exemption** ($13.99 million per person in 2025) — they file a gift tax return (Form 709) to track it, but no actual tax is due unless their cumulative lifetime gifts exceed that enormous threshold.

## Gifts of Appreciated Property

If you receive a gift of stock or other property (rather than cash), you generally take the giver's original cost basis — called "carryover basis" — rather than the stepped-up basis you'd get from an inheritance. If you later sell the asset, you'll owe capital gains tax on the appreciation from the **original** purchase price, including appreciation that occurred before you owned it.

## Example

A grandmother gives her grandson 100 shares of stock she bought years ago for $2,000, now worth $12,000. The grandson owes no tax on receiving the gift. If he sells the stock the next day for $12,000, he owes capital gains tax on a $10,000 gain ($12,000 − $2,000 basis) — the same gain his grandmother would have owed had she sold it herself.

## Gifts From Foreign Persons

If you receive gifts or inheritances from a foreign individual or estate exceeding **$100,000** in a year, you must report it on **Form 3520**, even though it's still not taxable income — this is a reporting requirement, not a tax.

## What Doesn't Count as a Taxable "Gift"

- Wedding gifts and holiday gifts from family — not taxable, no reporting needed unless cumulatively very large from one person
- Employer gifts and bonuses — these ARE taxable, because they're compensation, not personal gifts
- Tuition or medical expenses paid directly to a school or provider on your behalf — unlimited and excluded from gift tax entirely, separate from the annual exclusion
