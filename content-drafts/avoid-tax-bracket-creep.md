## Two Very Different Things Share the Same Name

"Bracket creep" refers to two separate phenomena that both deserve attention: pure **inflationary** bracket creep (your pay rises with inflation but brackets don't move, so your real purchasing power stays flat while your nominal tax rate climbs) and **real** bracket creep (your actual raise or income growth pushes more income into a higher marginal rate). The IRS largely solved the first problem; the second is just math you can plan around.

## Inflation-Indexed Brackets Solve Most of the Old Problem

Since 1985, federal tax brackets, the standard deduction, and most credit thresholds are adjusted annually for inflation using a Chained CPI formula. This means a cost-of-living raise that exactly matches inflation generally should **not** push you into a meaningfully higher effective rate, because the brackets moved up with it.

## Where Creep Still Bites: Non-Indexed Thresholds

Not everything adjusts for inflation. The Social Security benefit taxation thresholds ($25,000/$34,000 single, $32,000/$44,000 MFJ) have been fixed since the 1980s and 1993 respectively — meaning more retirees get pushed into higher taxable-benefit tiers every year purely from nominal income growth, with no corresponding inflation adjustment. The Additional Medicare Tax threshold ($200,000 single / $250,000 MFJ) is similarly fixed and has never been adjusted since its introduction in 2013.

## Strategies to Blunt Real Bracket Creep

- **Increase pre-tax retirement contributions as your pay rises** — keeping your taxable income growing more slowly than your gross pay
- **Time bonus and discretionary income** — if you have any control over bonus timing near year-end, review whether deferring into January helps avoid crossing a bracket threshold in the current year
- **Use tax-loss harvesting** to offset gains that would otherwise push taxable income higher
- **Max out HSA contributions** if eligible — one of the few accounts that reduces taxable income without any income-based phase-out for most savers

## A Worked Example

A single filer's taxable income sits at $102,000 — just under the $103,350 line separating the 22% and 24% brackets for 2025. A $2,000 raise pushes taxable income to $104,000, meaning only about $650 of that raise is taxed at 24% instead of 22% — a difference of about $13 in extra tax on that sliver, not a cliff. The myth that crossing this line costs more than it's worth is almost always wrong under marginal taxation.

## Where Phase-Outs Act Like Bracket Creep

Separate from brackets, phase-outs for the Child Tax Credit, Roth IRA eligibility, and the Premium Tax Credit (ACA subsidies) all step down as income rises — sometimes creating a steeper effective cost per dollar earned than the bracket system itself. These deserve as much attention as the headline bracket numbers when projecting the real cost of additional income.
