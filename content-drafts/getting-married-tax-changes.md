## The Day After "I Do," Your Tax Life Changes

The IRS doesn't care when during the year you got married — if you're legally married on December 31, you're treated as married for the **entire** tax year. That single rule drives most of what changes: your filing status, your brackets, and often your paycheck withholding.

## Filing Status Options

Once married, you can no longer file as Single. Your choices become:

- **Married Filing Jointly (MFJ)** — combines both incomes on one return; eligible for the widest range of credits and the largest standard deduction
- **Married Filing Separately (MFS)** — rarely beneficial, but sometimes used to protect one spouse from the other's tax liabilities or student loan income-driven repayment calculations

The vast majority of couples come out ahead filing jointly.

## The "Marriage Bonus" vs. the "Marriage Penalty"

Combining incomes doesn't always help or hurt in the same direction:

| Situation | Typical Effect |
|---|---|
| One spouse earns much more than the other | **Marriage bonus** — lower earner's income effectively gets taxed at a lower combined rate |
| Both spouses earn similar, high incomes | **Marriage penalty** — combined income pushes more dollars into higher brackets than two single returns would |

For 2025, the MFJ standard deduction is **$30,000** — exactly double the single filer's $15,000 — which neutralizes some but not all of the penalty at high incomes, since the upper tax brackets for MFJ aren't always double the single thresholds.

## Updating Your W-4

After the wedding, both spouses should submit a new **Form W-4** to their employers. Check "Married filing jointly" status and use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator, especially if both of you work — Step 2 of the W-4 (Multiple Jobs Worksheet) exists specifically to prevent under-withholding when two incomes combine.

## Example: Two Earners, Different Incomes

Say Spouse A earns $80,000 and Spouse B earns $35,000. Filing separately as singles, Spouse A's income would reach the 22% bracket. Filing jointly, the combined $115,000 (before deductions) is taxed using MFJ brackets, where the 22% bracket doesn't start until $96,950 of taxable income — meaning more of the household's money is taxed at 10% and 12% than if Spouse A filed alone.

## Other Things That Change

- **Name and address changes**: Notify the Social Security Administration if you changed your name — a mismatch between your SSN records and tax return name can delay your refund
- **Health insurance**: You may be able to add a spouse to your employer plan during a special enrollment period
- **Retirement accounts**: Spousal IRA contributions become available even if one spouse has little or no earned income
- **Beneficiary designations**: Update 401(k), life insurance, and IRA beneficiaries
