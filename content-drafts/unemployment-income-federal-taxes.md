## Form 1099-G Is the Document to Watch For

If you collected unemployment benefits at any point in 2025, your state workforce agency will issue you a **Form 1099-G** by January 31 of the following year, showing the total benefits paid in Box 1. That entire amount is reportable as federal taxable income on your Form 1040 — there is no exclusion, no matter your income level.

## Why This Surprises People

Unemployment benefits are often perceived as a "safety net" payment rather than "income," but the IRS treats it just like wages for federal tax purposes (though it is **not** subject to Social Security or Medicare tax, unlike wages). The confusion is understandable: during 2020–2021, a temporary COVID-era exclusion made up to $10,200 of unemployment tax-free for one year only — that exclusion expired and does not apply to 2025 benefits.

## Voluntary Withholding: Form W-4V

Because most states don't automatically withhold tax from unemployment checks, you have to opt in using **Form W-4V (Voluntary Withholding Request)**. If approved, a **flat 10%** is withheld from each unemployment payment for federal taxes — there's no partial-percentage option.

| Approach | Result |
|---|---|
| File Form W-4V, elect 10% withholding | Smaller biweekly checks, smaller tax bill in April |
| Don't file Form W-4V | Full benefit amount now, full tax liability due by April 15 |
| Make quarterly estimated payments instead | More control, but requires discipline and calculation |

## State Tax Treatment Varies

Unlike the federal government, **not every state taxes unemployment benefits**. States with no income tax (like Texas, Florida, and Washington) obviously don't tax it, but even some states with an income tax — such as California, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Virginia — exempt unemployment compensation specifically. Check your specific state's rules rather than assuming.

## Interaction With Other Tax Credits

Unemployment income counts toward your **Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)**, which can affect eligibility for other benefits:

- It counts as income for **Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)** income limits, even though it's not "earned income" itself for calculating the credit amount
- It can affect **ACA marketplace subsidy** eligibility if you're also buying your own health insurance
- It's included when calculating whether your **Social Security benefits** become taxable, if you're also receiving those

## Example

Someone collects $14,000 in unemployment benefits over the year with no withholding elected, plus $22,000 in wages from a job earlier in the year. Their federal taxable income includes the full $36,000 combined (minus the standard deduction). If they expected a refund based only on the $22,000 wage withholding, the added unemployment income with zero withholding often converts that expected refund into a balance due.
