## A Retirement Plan Built for Public Servants

A **457(b) plan** is offered primarily by state and local government employers (and certain nonprofit organizations, in a less common "non-governmental" form). It looks a lot like a 401(k) on the surface — pre-tax or Roth payroll contributions, tax-deferred growth — but has two features that set it apart.

## No Early Withdrawal Penalty

This is the single biggest difference: governmental 457(b) plans have **no 10% early withdrawal penalty**, even if you take a distribution before age 59½ — as long as you've actually separated from service. Ordinary income tax still applies, but you avoid the extra penalty that hits early 401(k) or IRA withdrawals. This makes 457(b)s unusually attractive for people planning early retirement.

## You Can Max Out Both a 457(b) AND a 401(k)/403(b)

If your employer offers both a 457(b) and a 401(k) or 403(b) (common for some state and municipal employees), the contribution limits are **separate** — you could contribute $23,500 to each in 2025, for $47,000 total pre-tax savings, far more than someone with access to only one plan type.

## Contribution Limits for 2025

| Category | Limit |
|---|---|
| Employee deferral (under 50) | $23,500 |
| Employee deferral (50+) | $31,000 |
| Special 3-year "final 3 years" catch-up | Up to $47,000 (in lieu of age-50 catch-up) |

## The Special "Final 3 Years" Catch-Up

457(b) plans offer a unique catch-up for participants in the **three years before their plan's normal retirement age**, allowing them to contribute up to double the normal limit ($47,000 in 2025) to make up for years they under-contributed earlier in their career. You cannot use this *and* the standard age-50 catch-up in the same year — your plan administrator applies whichever is larger.

## Non-Governmental 457(b)s Carry More Risk

If your 457(b) is offered by a nonprofit (rather than a government entity), plan assets remain the **employer's property** until distributed, exposing your balance to the employer's creditors if it becomes insolvent — unlike governmental 457(b)s, 401(k)s, and 403(b)s, which must hold assets in trust for employees.

## Worked Example

A 58-year-old city employee planning to retire at 60, three years from their plan's normal retirement age, could use the special catch-up to contribute up to $47,000 in 2025 instead of the standard $31,000 — an extra $16,000 in pre-tax savings in a single year.
