## The US Taxes You No Matter Where You Live

Unlike almost every other country, the United States taxes based on **citizenship**, not residency. If you're a U.S. citizen or green card holder living abroad, you generally must still file a federal tax return every year — even if you never set foot in the U.S. and pay full tax to your host country.

## The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)

The primary relief valve is the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion**. For 2025, you can exclude up to **$130,000** of foreign-earned income from U.S. federal tax, provided you meet either:

- The **Physical Presence Test**: present in a foreign country for at least 330 full days in a 12-month period, or
- The **Bona Fide Residence Test**: established genuine residency in a foreign country for an uninterrupted period including a full calendar year

## The Foreign Tax Credit (FTC) — Often Better for High Earners

Instead of the FEIE, you can claim the **Foreign Tax Credit**, which gives you a dollar-for-dollar credit for income taxes paid to a foreign government. For expats in high-tax countries, the FTC often eliminates U.S. tax liability entirely — better than the FEIE's capped exclusion — especially for income above the $130,000 exclusion limit. You generally cannot double-dip the same income with both.

## FBAR and FATCA: The Reporting Traps

Even if you owe zero U.S. tax, you may still have reporting obligations:

| Requirement | Trigger | Penalty for Missing |
|---|---|---|
| **FBAR** (FinCEN Form 114) | Foreign bank accounts totaling over $10,000 at any point in the year | Up to $10,000+ per violation (steeper for willful failures) |
| **FATCA** (Form 8938) | Foreign financial assets above certain thresholds (higher for those abroad) | Up to $10,000, additional penalties for continued non-filing |

These are separate from your income tax return and have severe penalties even when no tax is actually owed.

## Social Security and Self-Employment Tax Abroad

The FEIE excludes income from *income* tax, but **not** from self-employment tax if you're self-employed abroad — many expats are surprised to still owe the full 15.3% self-employment tax even while excluding the income itself. The U.S. has **Social Security agreements (totalization agreements)** with many countries to prevent double social security taxation for employees, but these don't automatically cover self-employed individuals.

## State Taxes May Follow You Too

If you don't properly sever ties with your former state (see our guide on moving between states), some states — particularly California and Virginia — may still consider you a resident for state tax purposes even after you've moved abroad, taxing your worldwide income until you clearly establish you've left for good.

## Example

An American earns $150,000 working remotely from Portugal, qualifying for the Physical Presence Test. Using the FEIE, $130,000 is excluded from U.S. tax; the remaining $20,000 is still subject to U.S. tax, though the Foreign Tax Credit for Portuguese taxes paid on that portion often offsets most or all of the remaining U.S. liability.
