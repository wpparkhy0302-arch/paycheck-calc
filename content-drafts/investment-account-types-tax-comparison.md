## Three Buckets, Three Tax Treatments

Every investment account you hold falls into one of three tax categories: **taxable**, **tax-deferred**, or **tax-free**. Understanding which bucket your money sits in determines how — and when — it gets taxed.

## Taxable Brokerage Accounts

A standard brokerage account offers no special tax treatment. Dividends and interest are taxed in the year received; selling an investment at a profit triggers capital gains tax (short- or long-term, depending on holding period). The upside: no contribution limits, and no penalty for withdrawing at any age.

## Tax-Deferred Accounts

Traditional 401(k)s and Traditional IRAs let contributions and growth sit untaxed until withdrawal. You typically get an upfront deduction (for IRAs) or pre-tax payroll contribution (for 401(k)s), and pay ordinary income tax only when you take money out — ideally in retirement, when your tax bracket may be lower.

## Tax-Free Accounts

Roth 401(k)s and Roth IRAs use after-tax contributions, but qualified withdrawals — including all growth — are never taxed again. Health Savings Accounts (HSAs) go even further: tax-deductible contributions, tax-free growth, **and** tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses — a rare triple tax advantage.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| Account Type | Contribution | Growth | Withdrawal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taxable brokerage | After-tax | Taxed annually (dividends/gains) | Capital gains tax on profit |
| Traditional 401(k)/IRA | Pre-tax | Tax-deferred | Ordinary income tax |
| Roth 401(k)/IRA | After-tax | Tax-deferred | Tax-free (if qualified) |
| HSA | Pre-tax | Tax-deferred | Tax-free (if medical) |

## Worked Example: $10,000 Growing to $20,000

If that growth happens in a taxable account and you're in the 15% long-term capital gains bracket, you'd owe **$1,500** when you sell. In a Roth account, you'd owe **$0**. In a Traditional account, you'd owe ordinary income tax on the full $20,000 withdrawal — potentially more than $1,500 depending on your bracket at withdrawal.

## Asset Location Strategy

Sophisticated investors practice "asset location" — placing tax-inefficient investments (bonds, REITs, actively-traded funds generating frequent short-term gains) inside tax-deferred or tax-free accounts, while holding tax-efficient investments (index funds, individual stocks held long-term) in taxable accounts where the preferential capital gains rate already minimizes drag.

## A Practical Priority Order

Many financial planners suggest: (1) contribute enough to your 401(k) to capture the full employer match, (2) max out an HSA if eligible, (3) max out a Roth or Traditional IRA, (4) return to maxing your 401(k), (5) invest additional savings in a taxable brokerage account.
