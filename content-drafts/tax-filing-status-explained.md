## Why Filing Status Matters More Than You'd Think

Your filing status isn't just a checkbox at the top of Form 1040 — it determines your standard deduction, which tax brackets apply to your income, your eligibility for several credits, and even whether you can contribute to certain retirement accounts. Two people with identical income can owe thousands of dollars apart in federal tax purely because of the box they checked.

## The Five Filing Statuses

**Single** — You're unmarried, divorced, or legally separated as of December 31, and don't qualify for Head of Household. 2025 standard deduction: $15,000.

**Married Filing Jointly (MFJ)** — You and your spouse combine income, deductions, and credits on one return. Almost always the cheapest option for married couples. 2025 standard deduction: $30,000.

**Married Filing Separately (MFS)** — Each spouse files their own return. Rarely beneficial on net, but useful in specific situations like income-driven student loan repayment or separating liability. Standard deduction: $15,000, and many credits (Earned Income Credit, education credits) are disallowed entirely.

**Head of Household (HOH)** — For unmarried taxpayers who paid more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying child or relative. Wider brackets and a bigger standard deduction than Single: $22,500 in 2025.

**Qualifying Surviving Spouse** — Available for up to two years after a spouse's death if you have a dependent child, letting you use MFJ brackets while filing as unmarried.

## How the IRS Determines Your Status

Your marital status is locked in as of **December 31** of the tax year — even if you married on December 30 or divorced on January 2, the IRS treats you as having held that status for the entire year.

## A Worked Comparison

A single parent earning $58,000 with one qualifying child:
- As **Single**: taxable income = $58,000 − $15,000 = $43,000, landing partly in the 22% bracket
- As **Head of Household**: taxable income = $58,000 − $22,500 = $35,500, with more of that income taxed at 10–12% before reaching 22%

The HOH status alone can save this filer several hundred dollars, separate from any Child Tax Credit.

## Choosing Between MFJ and MFS

| Factor | MFJ | MFS |
|---|---|---|
| Standard deduction | $30,000 combined | $15,000 each |
| EITC / education credits | Allowed | Disallowed |
| Liability for spouse's tax debts | Shared | Separated |
| Student loan income-driven plans | Combined income used | Often lower payment |

Most couples save money filing jointly. MFS mainly makes sense when one spouse has a large tax liability, past-due debts, or an income-driven student loan payment calculated on individual rather than combined income.

## Common Mistakes

- Filing Single when you actually qualify for the more generous Head of Household status
- Assuming you must file MFS just because you're going through a divorce that hasn't finalized by year-end
- Not revisiting filing status every year — a status that made sense last year may not this year
