## Three Separate Tax Events Happen at Death

When someone dies, their tax situation doesn't end in one simple step — there are actually up to three distinct filings involved, each with its own rules.

## 1. The Final Individual Return

A **final Form 1040** must be filed for the deceased, covering income earned from January 1 through the date of death. This is filed by the executor or surviving spouse, using the normal individual tax rules, due by the normal April 15 deadline of the following year (not accelerated by the death itself).

## 2. The Estate's Income Tax Return

If the estate itself earns income **after** death (such as interest on a bank account before it's distributed, or rental income from a property still in probate), the estate may need to file **Form 1041** (U.S. Income Tax Return for Estates and Trusts) for that income, separate from the deceased's personal final return.

## 3. The Estate Tax Return (Only for Large Estates)

As covered in detail in our estate tax guide, **Form 706** is only required if the estate exceeds the federal exemption ($13.99 million in 2025) — or if the executor wants to elect **portability**, allowing a surviving spouse to use any unused exemption, which requires filing Form 706 even for estates well below the threshold.

## Life Insurance Proceeds

Life insurance death benefits paid to a named beneficiary are generally **completely income-tax-free** at the federal level. However, if the policy was owned by the deceased personally (rather than, say, an irrevocable life insurance trust), the payout **is** included in their taxable estate for estate tax purposes — relevant only for very large estates.

## Trusts Can Avoid Probate — But Not Taxes Automatically

A **revocable living trust** helps assets pass to heirs without going through probate court, saving time and keeping the estate private. But a revocable trust provides **no estate or income tax savings** on its own — the assets are still counted in the deceased's taxable estate, and the trust's income is reported on the grantor's return while they're alive. Tax savings generally require **irrevocable** trust structures, set up well in advance with professional guidance.

## Step-Up in Basis Still Applies

As covered in our inheritance guide, assets passing through an estate still receive a **stepped-up cost basis** to fair market value at death — this is one of the most valuable features of the U.S. tax code for families with appreciated assets, and it applies whether assets pass via will, trust, or beneficiary designation.

## Beneficiary Designations Override Wills

Retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death bank accounts pass directly to named beneficiaries **regardless of what your will says**. Keeping these designations updated after marriage, divorce, or a death in the family is one of the simplest and most overlooked parts of estate planning.

## Example

An executor manages an estate with a $5 million taxable value. No federal estate tax is owed (well under the $13.99 million exemption), but the executor still files a final Form 1040 for the deceased's last year of life, and a Form 1041 for the $8,000 of interest the estate's bank account earned during the 6 months probate took to complete.
