## File Anyway — It's the Single Most Important Step

The biggest mistake taxpayers make when they can't pay is not filing at all. The **failure-to-file penalty** (5% of unpaid tax per month, up to 25%) is ten times steeper than the **failure-to-pay penalty** (0.5% per month). File your return on time — or file an extension — even if you can't send a dime with it.

## Your Options, Roughly in Order of Preference

**1. Pay what you can now, even partially.** Any payment reduces the balance that interest and the failure-to-pay penalty accrue against. Partial payment plus a payment plan for the rest beats doing nothing.

**2. Short-term payment plan (180 days or less).** If you owe less than $100,000 combined tax, penalties, and interest, the IRS generally approves this automatically online with no setup fee.

**3. Long-term installment agreement.** For balances under $50,000, the IRS offers streamlined online approval for monthly payments spread over up to 72 months. Setup fees range roughly $0–$178 depending on whether you apply online, use direct debit, and your income level (low-income taxpayers can have the fee waived).

**4. Offer in Compromise (OIC).** Lets you settle your tax debt for **less than the full amount owed**, but only if the IRS determines you genuinely cannot pay the full balance even through an installment plan, based on your income, expenses, and asset equity. Approval rates are moderate — the IRS's OIC pre-qualifier tool on irs.gov gives a reasonable sense of eligibility before you apply.

**5. Temporary delay (Currently Not Collectible status).** If paying anything would create genuine hardship, the IRS can temporarily pause collection — interest and penalties still accrue, but garnishment and levies stop until your situation improves.

## What Happens If You Ignore It

Ignoring a balance due doesn't make it disappear. The IRS can eventually file a **federal tax lien** against your property, issue a **levy** on wages or bank accounts, or offset future refunds against the debt. These are the IRS's last resorts — they typically arrive only after multiple unanswered notices, not immediately after one missed deadline.

## Interest and Penalties Keep Compounding

Interest accrues daily on the unpaid balance at the federal short-term rate plus 3%, compounded daily — meaning a balance you ignore for a year can grow by roughly 8% beyond what you originally owed, separate from any penalty.

## A Worked Example

You owe $4,000 and can pay $1,000 now. Paying that $1,000 immediately and setting up a 24-month installment agreement on the remaining $3,000 limits further penalty accrual to the smaller remaining balance, versus letting penalties and interest compound on the full $4,000 while you decide what to do.
